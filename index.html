Power8Board

Power board for an eight-thruster ROV

EECS

Overview

Power8Board is a custom power distribution and sensing PCB intended for underwater robotics applications (e.g. ROVs / AUVs). I created this board from the ground up to support a change from six to eight thrusters for Hydra (Jesuit HS Robotics’ 25-26 ROV). Having eight thrusters was a project I led as CTO, which meant I not only was involved in the electrical side of things, but with mechanical and software integration. I believed this change was necessary, as an eight-thruster configuration would provide Hydra with 6 DoF (degrees of freedom), giving it unprecedented maneuverability and control.

Design

The board converts 48 V DC down to 5 V and 12 V, distributes up to ~100 amps of power to eight ESCs which control/power the eight thrusters, and provides per-thruster current measurement and voltage rail monitoring (3.3, 5, 12, 48) over I²C.

To step down 48 V DC to 12 V, choosing the right DC-DC converters mattered since 12 V were used to power all the thrusters and ROV tools. To guide my decision-making, I drafted these primary requirements:

  1. Provide over 700 W. This is because pairing two would maximize our 48 V 30 A supply.
  2. Have a minimum voltage requirement that is no greater than 42 V. Even though 48 V is our supply, there is a voltage drop across our tether.
  3. Be in a quarter-brick footprint. This footprint would fit in our electronics enclosure while being large enough to perform optimally.
  4. Have a mountable heat-sink. This is for heat dissipation as we can leverage the passive cooling of the surrounding water when thermally coupling the converters to the aluminum enclosure.
  5. Have a double-pin layout. This is to prevent concentrated heat spots on the PCB and is what allowed me to use pin-receptacles on the board. I wanted to use pin-receptacles because it would allow me to swap the power converters.

With these requirements, I chose to use these 860 W 48-12 V Murata converters. Conversion to 5 V used this Murata 48-5 V converter that we had used with no issues in previous years.

While just routing the pins of these DC-DC converters would suffice, I wanted to go the extra mile and understand what was really happening when these electronics were under load. Specifically, I wanted real-time current readings from each of the eight thrusters, and voltage readings for all the power rails.

To achieve current monitoring, I chose to use eight INA240 current sensors, one for each thruster. The INA240 was an analog sensor that would amplify the voltage drop across a shunt and have its output routed to an ADC which would communicate with a Raspberry Pi via I²C. I opted for this mixed-signal design as the individual analog sensors would be easy to route, while the ADCs would provide the signal integrity and communication necessary for real-time monitoring with a Raspberry Pi. Voltage monitoring was accomplished through voltage dividers whose outputs would go to an ADC and then be appropriately scaled in software.

For a more in-depth technical breakdown, check out the files and README on my github.

Build

Using the gerber files generated from my design in KiCad, I ordered the PCB from JLCPCB. When designing the board, I sourced all the components from Digikey and TME. For organization, I inputted manufacturer, datasheet, part #, price, and order link information for every component to generate an interactive BOM using a KiCad 3rd party extension. This made ordering components seamless.

I also used the interactive BOM for manufacturing, as highlighting any component in the rows on the left, would show where it is on the PCB (red boxes on the right). This saved time, but also made sure I didn’t place components in the wrong spot (especially for those ceramic capacitors that look identical).

For soldering the SMD components, I used a stainless steel stencil to apply solder paste and used a hot plate. All the SMD components are on one side which makes soldering really easy with the hot plate. After this, I soldered all the through-hole components with my soldering iron.

Test

Testing was stressful, but was done methodically to identify any potential issues with the board. I first plugged in the power converters and got the proper voltage readings out of them. I then plugged in one ESC which was connected to a thruster testbench. With a servo controller that fed a signal to the ESC, the thruster ran successfully, pulling 180 W. I then scaled up and tested with multiple thrusters. After this, I tested the current and voltage sensing. I was quite surprised that version 1 of my board worked so well. Still, there were a few changes I wanted to make:

  1. Changing the enabling resistors for the converters from 10k Ω to 100 Ω since the converters weren’t turning on at 10k Ω.
  2. Splitting the 12 V plane into left and right banks since I found current sharing between the converters was unnecessary.

These changes were applied to version 2 of the board.

SuperHAT

Raspberry Pi 4/5 HAT for ROV applications

EECS

Overview

SuperHAT is a HAT (hardware attached on top) that expands the capabilities of the Raspberry Pi 4/5. In my junior year, I designed this PCB from the ground up since the Raspberry Pi in our ROV (Jesuit Robotics) needed to control 5-12 V tools, interface with sensors and controllers via I²C, and drive a variety of motors. This was my first ever “major” PCB project, so I learned a lot from this experience.

Design

SuperHAT’s key features are:

  1. Four N-Type MOSFETs for GPIO switching 5 or 12 V tools
  2. One dual H-bridge motor driver
  3. One stepper motor driver
  4. Two 5 V servo connectors
  5. Overvoltage protection
  6. Voltage & current sensing

To route all the traces needed for these features while keeping costs reasonable, I opted for a 4-layer stackup (SIG-PWR-GND-SIG).

Over the past two years (24-25’ & 25-26’), SuperHAT’s MOSFETs have powered tools such as LEDs for morse code signaling, bilge pumps to safely collect fish species, peristaltic pumps to retrieve saline fluid, and more. The I²C connectors have been used to interface with depth sensors, thruster control boards, IMUs, etc. SuperHAT has also driven a servo motor that adjusts the angle of a camera and a magnetically-coupled motor that can turn valves in either direction.

More specific technical details on how these features were implemented can be found on my github.

Build

The manufacturing process for SuperHAT is identical to Power8Board. I created an interactive BOM that aids me in ordering and manufacturing SuperHAT. Soldering for SMD components is done via a stencil and hot plate and the through hole components are done by hand. All of the sourcing and manufacturing was done by myself.

Test

To test SuperHAT with a Raspberry Pi 4/5, I wrote code in Python that runs each of its functions. Some of the libraries for testing sensors were provided by Adafruit and Blue Robotics. SuperHAT V1 had some issues, most notably a faulty pull-down setup at the gate of the MOSFETs. This was remedied in version 2 of the board. For version 3, I mentored an underclassman by walking him through the circuits and writing guidelines for the revisions, and he designed the board himself.

Float

Underwater profiling data collection device

EECSME

Float Overview

A float is a vertically profiling device that changes its buoyancy to ascend and descend the water depths. While profiling, it reads from its sensors to collect data. Floats in the real world go thousands of feet deep to retrieve vital oceanographic data such as carbon dioxide levels, salinity, and temperature. This data helps scientists/researchers better understand the effects of climate change in our ocean ecosystems.

P.O.D, 2023-24

Design & Build

In my sophomore year, I wanted to redesign the float from the ground up since the prior year’s version had many issues caused by its syringe-based design. To fix these issues, I came up with the idea of using a peristaltic pump. To test this idea, I built a prototype and discovered it was twice as efficient and would be way more serviceable. This is because there is much less friction with a peristaltic pump vs. an O-ring rubbing on a tube. Additionally, a peristaltic only needs a couple of tubes and a bladder. This is far easier to design and service than a syringe which requires a precise seal, lead screw, and motor.

Mechanically, I designed P.O.D in CAD as three sections. The flanges were machined from Type 2 PVC on a CNC mill from CAM I programmed in Fusion 360, and the polycarbonate tubes were cut on a bandsaw. I epoxied the flanges to the tubes and face-sealed the housings with 2.62 mm O-rings. Inside, an RP2040 reads a Blue Robotics depth sensor, drives the pump, and sends data to the mission station over LoRa once the float surfaces.

I designed and manufactured the prototype electronics using a protoboard. I used a buck converter that converted 9V to 5V to power the RP2040. A COTS dual H-bridge motor driver was used to power the pump and the depth sensor was connected via I²C. Later, these prototype electronics got condensed into a PCB with the help of an electronics member.

To profile, I used the depth sensor instead of relying on fixed time increments to drive the pump. Here’s the logic: the pump would suck in water to sink and once it was about halfway down, it would shut off and coast. Once it is near the bottom, the pump reverses to push out water, making it float and halfway up, it would shut off and coast. When it reaches the surface, it would send the depth data it collected over the profile. Using this profiling algorithm not only saved battery life, but also prevented drift. I wrote this code in Arduino C.

The first ever PCB I made was a mission station controller HAT (hardware attached on top) for the float. One button is to manually pump out water, one is for manually pumping in water, and one initiates a profile.

Testing

After getting all the components of the float together, I began profiling the float to see if there were any more issues. Everything was going smoothly, and it completed over 50 profiles. One evening, a week before it was going to get shipped, the float would not come back up. Suspecting something wrong, I brought it up with a long stick 13 ft from the pool and saw the pump continuing to inflate the very full bag. Ultimately, before I was able to shut it down, the pressure inside the sealed housings blew off one of the epoxy seals. After removing the old epoxy with the lathe and a knife, I was able to re-epoxy the flange to the polycarbonate tube just before it shipped off. It won full points at the MATE ROV World Championship.

GO-POD, 2024-25

Design & Build

Junior year, the objective changed: instead of profiling all the way to the bottom, the floats needed to hold at 2.5 meters for 45 seconds. While I could’ve reused last year’s float and just modified the profiling logic by adding a control algorithm, I wanted to improve on the whole design.

For the mechanical design, I worked with a mechanical member of the Jesuit Robotics team. We collaborated over whiteboard discussions on the internal layout of the components (pump, electronics, bag, batteries) and housing design. These collaborative and creative brainstorming sessions are my favorite part of the making process.

Compared to sophomore year’s float which had three housings, we decided to migrate to a single Blue Robotics housing. This was because the internals could be accessed without removing screws. This greatly reduced servicing time. We could not manufacture this type of housing in our team’s shop, due to not having the machines that could hold the tolerances required for a reliable seal. Despite this, the end caps were CNC’d out of smoked polycarbonate. Improvements in the internal layout were placing the electronics at the top to protect them in case of a pump or tube leak. Another advantage of placing the electronics up top is that it is closer to the depth sensor and antenna, requiring shorter cable lengths, improving signal integrity.

Unlike the previous year, I designed all the electronics by myself. There are two custom boards inside GO-POD. One serves as a daughter board for the ESP32, giving it the functionality to drive the pump, read the depth sensor, and drive an indicating light. The other serves as a power board where eight 9V rechargeable Ni-MH batteries are connected in parallel. I decided to switch to an ESP32 from an RP2040 as LoRa’s range proved unnecessary and sending data over Wifi proved easier.

To begin profiling, the control station sends a command through WiFi to a web server on the ESP32. When the ESP32 receives the command, it begins profiling to the targeted depth of 2.5 m. To maintain this depth, a PID program is used to determine the pump’s direction. When GO-POD resurfaces, the control station reconnects with the ESP32’s web server, retrieves the collected depth data, and generates a graph for analysis. The source code can be found here on my github.

PCB Medals

Creative math competition medals

EE

Since my school was hosting the January Mathletes competition in 2025, I thought it would be special if the trophies weren’t in their traditional form (those plasticky ones that always break). Furthermore, a medal that everyone in the top 3 finishing teams could take home would be more memorable. I designed these PCB medals with a creative math twist. 1st place corresponds to phi, 2nd place to e, and 3rd place to pi. Mathematically, the floors of each of these special numbers equate to 1, 2, 3, respectively. White LEDs are used to illuminate these numbers on the front. On the back, a 2032 coin cell battery paired with an on/ off button (not momentary) can switch the LEDs. I made 45 of these in total, 15 for each top 3 finishish team.
